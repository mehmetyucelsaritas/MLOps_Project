The web-based service Brandrisk skog och mark (www.smhi.se/brandrisk) isa decision support tool that canbe used to assess fire risk and fire behavior. The service is primarily aimed at municipal rescue services, county administrative boards and the forestry industry and can, for example, be used for preventive fire protection measures, planning firefighting efforts and issuing fire bans. The value is calculated asa weighted average ofDMC andDC, with DMC being the most important. DC represents the moisture in thick compact organic soil layers (about 18cm), such asthe peat/ humus layer. The water storage inthe layer corresponds to about 100mm of water. When calculating FFMC, precipitation, relative humidity, wind speed, temperature and previous values for FFMC are taken into account. High values ofDC also mean an increased risk of lightning strikes during thunderstorms. Note that there maybe some delay (upto several days) ifthe upper fuel layers are initially moist. High values ofDMC mean an increased risk of intense and difficult-to-extinguish fires as well asan increased risk of lightning ignition during thunderstorms. FFMC represents the moisture inthe top layer (about 1cm) of finely divided fuel onthe ground surface, for example leaves, needles and the upper part ofthe moss/fern. DMC represents the moisture ina slightly deeper layer ofthe fuel onthe ground (about 7cm), for example the moss layer and the upper part ofthe humus layer. The water storage in this layer corresponds toup to about 15mm of water. Alow DC value indicates high humidity anda high value indicates drought (there isno upper limit, but the value is usually below 600 for Swedish conditions, except inthe latter part of really dry summers). The relative humidity is calculated based onthe air temperature and dew point ata height of2 meters, and indicates how much moisture content (water vapor) the air hasin relation tothe theoretical maximum possible (100% indicates completely saturated air, which can occur, for example, in rain orfog). Detection of forest fires
3.2 Forest fire detection in Sweden 3.1 General intervention while the fire is small has long been well proven when it comes to fighting forest fires. If forest fire extinguishing is started ata later stage, there isa risk that the fire has grown in size, developed a larger fire front and spread to vegetation with a worsening fire course. The extinguishing work then risks becoming very resource-intensive and that the rescue service risks ending up behind schedule in relation tothe fire. Thus, forest fire detection isa very important component inthe field of forest fire extinguishing. Already atthe endof the 19th century, organized forest fire detection began in Sweden, inthe form ofan expansion of forest fire towers. During the 1930s, the first trials of forest fire flights were carried outin Jämtland based on experiences from earlier trials in Finland and Canada. During this period and during the Second World War, the network of forest fire towers was also expanded ona large scale, toa nationwide system. After the Second World War, the fire function for forest fire towers south ofthe Karlstad–Gävle line was discontinued. The reason was that the number of fires detected in southern Sweden was very low. During the 1950s, trials and then implementation of forest fire flights began dueto the increasingly increased costs for forest fire towers. In line with the expansion of forest fire flights, forest fire towers were discontinued toa corresponding extent. During the 1960s, the last forest fire towers in Sweden were discontinued2 . The concept of early detection ofthe fire, combined with rapid first aid, In addition to human suffering, forest and land fires also cause costs for society and environmental effects – both positive and negative. Oneof the most important tools for mitigating the negative effects of forest fires is early detection ofthe fires. 3.3.1 Satellite detection 3.3 Additional detection means The term public generally includes all non-organized detection activities and can, for example, include tourists in forest areas, berry pickers, hunters, commercial aircraft, etc. Statistics from both Sweden3 and Canada4 show that the general public detects a majority of forest fires, even in areas with extensive forest fire surveillance aircraft. The proportion of fires detected by forest fire surveillance aircraft is inversely proportional to population density3, but with mobile telephony, the general public has most likely come to play an increasingly important role inthe early detection of forest fires, even in sparsely populated areas. Below are two examples of additional methods and are nota complete list of existing detection methods. Fora more comprehensive account of different detection methods, a feasibility study on future detection of vegetation and forest fires is recommended5 . Forest fire surveillance by forest fire aircraft is carried out along a number of predefined routes distributed around the country. The overall goal ofthe forest fire surveillance by aircraft function in Sweden isto detect and determine the position of forest fires as early as possible in order to thereby minimize the spread ofthe fire and thereby costly rescue efforts and major property losses. MSBis responsible for overall direction, financing, follow-upand final evaluation, and final reporting ofthe results ofthe operation. The County Administrative Board, which has production responsibility for forest fire surveillance by aircraft, decides within the framework ofMSB's guidelines, primarily whether surveillance by aircraft shall be conducted inthe county. The County Administrative Board then decides onthe scope ofthe surveillance, for example with regard tothe route ofthe flight routes and how often surveillance shall be carried out. The County Administrative Board also draws upa special plan for the surveillance, for monitoring the results inits county and for annual reporting. With the development of operational satellites for observing the Earth's surface, tools were provided for continuous scanning ofthe Earth's surface. Depending onthe orbit, observation technology and platform technology, information about the Earth's surface canbe provided within a very short period of time for most locations on Earth. Sensors on satellites canbe used, among other things, for observations ofthe Earth's vegetation - where data regarding the moisture content ofthe vegetation canbe included in fire risk models - but also for the detection of forest fires. Detection of forest fires using satellites has been occurring ina number of different countries such asthe USA, Australia and Finland for several years. In Sweden, satellite detection of forest fires has been tested for several years, but was put into operation in 2022. The first form istop fires where the spread of fire inthe higher fuel layers is dependent onthe fire inthe lower fuel layers, andif the fire inthe lower fuel layers stops or subsides, the fire inthe higher fuel layers will also stop. Among the most extreme fire processes that can occur ina forest fire isa fire plume- dominated fire process, i.e. a fire process where the fire plume itself has the strongest impact. Fora fire plume-dominated fire process to occur, a high fire load, very low fuel moisture content and unstable air masses are required. This type of fire process is associated with weaker winds than the corresponding wind- borne top fire – usually less than 8 meters/second – anda clear fire plume, also called a convection plume. Inthe second form, the power development from the fire inthe higher fuel layers isso high that itis completely independent ofthe fire inthe lower fuel layers. The fire hasa clear direction of spread anda fire area ofa tapered, elliptical character. In this type of forest fire, fire whirls and flying fires often occur; however, the throw distance ofthe flying fires isnot as long asin the fire plume- dominated variant. Wind speeds are often higher at ground level than higher up.
4.3.3 Fire whirls The fire plume-dominated fire progression continues fora maximum ofa number of hours and then ceases. This type of fire progression often starts inthe early afternoon when the relative humidity is lowest, the temperature is highest, the availability ofdry fuel is greatest and the probability of unstable air masses is greatest. The high fire plume means increased throw distances for aerial fires asthe plume (and burning or glowing material) reaches higher into the atmosphere. The plume-dominated fire progression burns with a higher power development but has a lower spread rate compared tothe wind-borne top fire. However, the violent combustions that can occur inthe plume-dominated fire progression still give rise to higher spread rates than inthe wind-borne top fire. The spread of fire ina plume-dominated fire progression is often difficult to predict, since sudden and violent fire spreads in widely different directions occur. The fire plume-dominated fire process spreads intwo different ways: firstly through convection and flame radiation from the fire plume, and secondly with the help of strong downward winds, which cause fire spread in different directions. The resulting wind speeds canbe very high and can topple or break trees. Any attempts to extinguish the fire during the plume-dominated fire process will most likely be futile and too risky. The best thing todo isto let the ground personnel retreat and wait for the fire process to stop and for more favorable weather conditions to arise. However, airborne resources with greater water capacity can possibly be deployed to protect buildings, for example. Emergency services personnel on site receive an early warning based onthe appearance and development ofthe fire plume, and color changes, strong growth and turbulent movements are clear warning signs. The fire plume canbe advantageously monitored from the air, where any changes canbe seen early and clearly. Any fire vortices inthe area can indicate unstable air masses and very low humidity levels can also serve asa warning signal andbe taken into account ina risk assessment. Fire whirls generally require unstable air masses and high fire load to occur. Fire whirls can, for example, occur in connection with a cold front passing over the fire area oron the leeward side ofa hill. The fire whirls themselves cause a strong suction ofair at ground level, tornado-like winds, increased effect development onthe fire, increased flame length, increased spread speed and aerial fires. The greatest risk with fire whirls isthe aerial fires that they spread inthe terrain ata shorter distance from the fire front. Fire whirls are usually less than one meter in diameter and then donot pose any major risks, but with increasing size the risks described above increase.
Since aerial fires can greatly worsen the course ofa fire and make extinguishing work more difficult, fighting them should be prioritized. Flying resources canbe used to advantage in fighting aerial fires. Aerial fires can occur asa result of fires inthe lower fuel layers but are more common asa result of fires inthe higher fuel layers and fires in piles of logging residues. The throw distance of aerial fires can vary greatly from afew meters to several kilometers. A flying fire isa forest fire that has arisen outside the main fire area and that may have arisen by burning or glowing material being carried bythe wind inthe direction ofthe fire's spread and subsequently igniting fuel onthe ground surface orin higher fuel layers. The glowing material maybe inthe form ofa leaf, a twig, a cone ora piece of bark. The actual ignition of fuel onthe ground surface orin higher fuel layers maybe delayed in time asthe fuel may need tobe dried out and heated upby the burning or glowing material. Some time delay can therefore be expected before the actual flying fire occurs. The occurrence of flying fires is usually a clear indicator of extreme forest fire events. The phenomenon of forest fires burning together (coalescence) isan example ofa very extreme fire process, perhaps even the most extreme one that canbe experienced ina forest fire. Separate forest fires influence and attract each other, both protective burns and counterfires as well asthe main fire itself. The larger and more intense the fire, the greater the impact ithas on more distant fires. The phenomenon can occur during a single forest fire where separate fire fronts are positioned against each other – orin forest fires and burning that have resulted in aerial fires ata shorter distance from the fire front and which have subsequently burned together with the forest fire with violent force. 4.3.5 Combustions 4.3.4 Aircraft fires

Night work
Night also brings with ita number of changed circumstances that are important to consider. Fire smoke isnot as easily observed visually as during the day. This means that any aerial fires that could have been detected during the day via the smoke column are usually detected via the flames. Fire-damaged trees that areat risk of falling maybe more difficult to detect. The risk of sprains and falls also increases during the dark hours ofthe day. Consider only working along existing boundary lines if there isa high risk of personal injury. Night is usually ideal for fighting a forest fire because the intensity ofthe fire decreases during the night. Humidity is usually highest at night, the wind has subsided and the temperature has dropped, which is beneficial for the physically demanding firefighting work. With the decreasing fire intensity, fires at night can often be fought with a direct attack, which is very beneficial from a firefighting perspective. Night can also be used for, for example, improving existing boundary lines, performing maintenance on engine sprayers, etc. There area number of different measures to facilitate work at night. In addition to helmets, headlamps or flashlights, staff should also wear light reflective vests. Consider using a handheld thermal camera to facilitate the detection of fire sources. Hazards, paths, escape routes, etc. canbe provided with reflective tape. If possible, night staff should have been inthe same area during the day to increase orientation. For example, shift changes can take place before darkness falls. Topographic maps or orientation maps are excellent material for drawing attention to risk areas for fall accidents, but also for seeing where boundary lines canbe laid, vehicles can move, etc. Night orientation is also an excellent exercise. 8. 11.8.1 Direct and indirect attacks 11.8 Waterbombing tactics If possible, the flying resources should be concentrated during the initial stage ofthe fire. This isto avoid tying up flying resources toa possible fire fora longer period of time. Itis better to concentrate early, getan early decision and detach the flying resources tobe prepared fora new alarm. During the initial stage, one should avoid using both helicopters and airplanes for water bombing asa mixof flying resources requires an organization that can take time toget in place. Direct attack means that the water bombardment is carried out directly onthe fire band orin the immediate vicinity ofthe fire band and then in close cooperation with the rescue service personnel onthe ground. For example, half the load canbe dropped onthe fire itself and the other half in front ofthe fire, this to achieve a speed and intensity reduction ofthe fire. Direct attack is used in frontal attacks, flank attacks, aerial fires and sudden flare-ups (see more about this below). The flying resources are mainly used for spot operations with the aim of creating time for the fire service, reducing the fire intensity and enabling ground operations. This canbe compared tothe effort onthe ground which aims to contain the fire, stop further fire spread, ensure that the areas closest tothe boundary lines are extinguished, etc. Helicopter water bombing tactics have some similarities tothe tactics used by ground rescue services, including using direct and indirect attacks. However, the tactics also differ dueto the different tasks. In some cases, rescue service personnel may direct a helicopter toa specific point inthe terrain. This canbe done in various ways, where a developed map with a grid system canbe used asa basis. One method isto direct the helicopter using characteristic landforms or vegetation, for example, “Northeast ofthe big rock, distance 150 meters”. The characteristic landform can also be called a “bullseye”. Another method isthe clock method. Straight ahead, the helicopter crew has12 o’clock, straight back has6 o’clock, tothe left has9 o’clock, etc. The helicopter is then directed by saying, for example, “Nine o’clock, distance 200 meters”. A third method isfor rescue service personnel to mark out the places where they want the water bombing to take place, for example with brightly colored markers. Indirect attack means that the felling is carried outon the fuel that is further away from the fire band inthe path ofthe forest fire, i.e. the fuel is wetted down, possibly in connection with fire streets. In Sweden, in most cases, it will only bea direct attack dueto the evaporation ofthe water and its decreasing effect over time. The text below will therefore focus mainly onthe direct attack.
